# PART: FUNDAMENTALS


## 1. A motorway speed limit, in physics units
`GCSE` · Units & conversions · Part 1 · Fundamentals (pp. 5-16)

**PROBLEM**

The UK motorway speed limit is 70 mph. Every physics equation you will use needs speed in metres per second. Convert it. Take 1 mile = 1609 m.

**KNOWN** — speed = 70 mph  |  1 mile = 1609 m  |  1 hour = 3600 s

**WANTED** — the same speed in m/s

**STEPS**

1. **Turn the miles into metres**  
   In one hour the car covers 70 miles.  
   `70 × 1609 m = 112 630 m`

2. **Turn the hour into seconds**  
   A speed is metres divided by seconds, so the hour has to go.  
   `112 630 m ÷ 3600 s = 31.29 m/s`

3. **Quote it sensibly**  
   The speed limit was given to 2 s.f., so 3 s.f. is as far as the data honestly reaches.  
   `v = 31.3 m/s`

**ANSWER — 31.3 m/s**  
70 mph is just over 31 metres every second.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 19.4 m/s**  
Dividing by 3.6. That converts km/h to m/s, not mph. Applied to miles per hour it throws away 38% of the speed. The “÷ 3.6” shortcut only becomes valid once the number is already in km/h.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
70 mph is about 112.6 km/h, and 112.6 ÷ 3.6 = 31.29 m/s. Two different routes, the same answer.


## 2. Density of an object you cannot measure with a ruler
`GCSE` · Quantities & units · Part 1 · Fundamentals (pp. 5-16)

**PROBLEM**

A stainless steel nut has a mass of 56.0 g. Dropped into a measuring cylinder, the water level rises from 50.0 cm³ to 57.0 cm³. Find its density in kg/m³.

**KNOWN** — mass = 56.0 g  |  water before = 50.0 cm³  |  water after = 57.0 cm³

**WANTED** — the density in kg/m³

**STEPS**

1. **The volume is the rise, not the final reading**  
   The nut pushes aside its own volume of water, so the volume you want is the difference.  
   `V = 57.0 − 50.0 = 7.0 cm³`

2. **Density = mass ÷ volume**  
   Work in the units you were given, then convert once, at the end.  
   `ρ = 56.0 ÷ 7.0 = 8.0 g/cm³`

3. **Convert to SI**  
   1 g = 1/1000 kg and 1 cm³ = 1/1 000 000 m³, so the two conversions do not cancel: they leave a factor of 1000 upwards.  
   `ρ = 8.0 × 1000 = 8000 kg/m³`

**ANSWER — 8000 kg/m³**  
8.0 g/cm³, which is 8000 kg/m³.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 8.0 kg/m³**  
Keeping the g/cm³ number and simply changing the label. The answer is then out by a factor of 1000 – and it should look absurd on sight, because 8.0 kg/m³ is lighter than air.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Stainless steel runs about 7900-8100 kg/m³. 8000 sits inside that range, so the answer describes a material that actually exists. That is the fastest check there is on a density.


## 3. Two tugs, one barge
`BOTH` · Vectors · Part 1 · Fundamentals (pp. 5-16)

**PROBLEM**

Two tugs pull a barge. One pulls with 8.0 kN due north, the other with 6.0 kN due east. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant force.

**KNOWN** — F north = 8.0 kN  |  F east = 6.0 kN  |  angle between them = 90 °

**WANTED** — the resultant force: magnitude and direction

**STEPS**

1. **Draw the two forces tip to tail**  
   They are at 90° to each other, so the two forces and the resultant form a right-angled triangle.  
   `the resultant is the hypotenuse`

2. **Pythagoras gives the magnitude**  
   This is the whole point of calling force a vector: you cannot simply add the numbers.  
   `F = √(8.0² + 6.0²) = √100 = 10.0 kN`

3. **Trigonometry gives the direction**  
   Measure it from north, the way a bearing is always quoted.  
   `θ = tan⁻¹(6.0 ÷ 8.0) = 36.9° east of north`

**ANSWER — 10.0 kN at 36.9° east of north**  
It is a 3-4-5 triangle scaled up by two, which is why the numbers come out clean.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 14 kN**  
Adding the magnitudes. That is only correct when two forces point the same way. Here it overstates the pull by 40% – and it produces no direction at all, which is the clue that the method itself is wrong.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
A resultant must always lie between the difference of the two forces (2.0 kN) and their sum (14.0 kN). 10.0 kN sits inside that window.


## 4. Reading a speed off a distance-time graph
`GCSE` · Graphs · Part 1 · Fundamentals (pp. 5-16)

**PROBLEM**

A distance-time graph is a straight line. At t = 2.0 s the distance is 14.0 m; at t = 6.0 s it is 34.0 m. Find the speed.

**KNOWN** — point A = (2.0 s, 14.0 m)  |  point B = (6.0 s, 34.0 m)

**WANTED** — the speed of the object

**STEPS**

1. **Speed is the gradient, not the height**  
   On a distance-time graph the gradient is distance per unit time, which is exactly what speed means.  
   `v = Δd ÷ Δt`

2. **Take the differences**  
   Use two points that are far apart; it keeps any reading error small.  
   `Δd = 34.0 − 14.0 = 20.0 m    and    Δt = 6.0 − 2.0 = 4.0 s`

3. **Divide**  
   `v = 20.0 ÷ 4.0 = 5.0 m/s`

**ANSWER — 5.0 m/s**  
Constant speed, because the line is straight.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 5.67 m/s**  
Taking a single point and dividing distance by time (34.0 ÷ 6.0). That only works if the line passes through the origin. This one does not – it starts at 4.0 m – so the single-point method comes out 13% too high.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Test it on the other point: the line predicts 4.0 + 5.0 × 2.0 = 14.0 m at t = 2.0 s, which is what the graph shows.

## 5. Where the Highway Code braking distance comes from
`GCSE` · Motion & acceleration · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A car travelling at 70 mph brakes hard, decelerating at 6.53 m/s². How far does it travel between the brakes biting and the car stopping?

**KNOWN** — u = 70 mph (see problem 1)  |  v = 0 m/s  |  a = −6.53 m/s²

**WANTED** — the braking distance s

**STEPS**

1. **Convert before you do anything else**  
   Every SUVAT equation is written in SI units, and mph is not one.  
   `u = 31.29 m/s`

2. **Choose the equation without t in it**  
   You are not asked for the time and you are not given it, so pick the equation that never mentions it.  
   `v² = u² + 2as`

3. **The car stops, so v = 0**  
   This is where most marks are lost: zero is the FINAL speed. The 70 mph is u, not v.  
   `0 = 31.29² + 2(−6.53)s`

4. **Rearrange, and mind the two minus signs**  
   Deceleration makes a negative, and it cancels against the minus from moving u² across. A positive distance is the sign that you did it right.  
   `s = −979.1 ÷ (2 × −6.53) = 979.1 ÷ 13.06 = 75.0 m`

**ANSWER — 75.0 m**  
About 75 metres – and that is only after the driver has already reacted.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 375 m**  
Putting 70 straight into SUVAT. Because the equation squares the speed, a unit slip does not merely scale the answer, it scales it by the square: the result comes out 5.0 times too large.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The Highway Code quotes a 75 m braking distance at 70 mph. This calculation reproduces it, which is the real lesson: the number in the booklet is physics, not a guess.


## 6. A stone dropped from a cliff
`GCSE` · Motion & acceleration · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A stone is dropped from a cliff 45 m above the sea. Ignoring air resistance, how long does it take to reach the water? Take g = 9.81 m/s².

**KNOWN** — s = 45.0 m  |  u = 0 m/s  |  a = 9.81 m/s²

**WANTED** — the time of fall t

**STEPS**

1. **“Dropped” means u = 0**  
   That one word is worth a mark. “Thrown” would not be.  
   `u = 0 m/s`

2. **Choose the equation linking s, u, a and t**  
   `s = ut + ½at²,   and with u = 0 this becomes s = ½at²`

3. **Rearrange for t**  
   `t = √(2s ÷ a) = √(2 × 45.0 ÷ 9.81) = √9.174`

4. **Solve**  
   `t = 3.03 s`

**ANSWER — 3.03 s**  
It lands after about three seconds, hitting the water at 29.7 m/s.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 4.59 s**  
Dividing the height by g. That treats 9.81 as a speed, when it is an acceleration – metres per second, per second. Dividing a distance by it does not produce a time at all; the units come out wrong, which is precisely how you catch this one before the examiner does.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The impact speed works out at 29.7 m/s, about 107 km/h. Fast, but entirely believable for a 45 m drop – roughly a fifteen-storey building.

**ANOTHER WAY IN**  
GCSE route. The equation s = ut + ½at² is not on the GCSE sheet, but this problem is still within reach. Use v² − u² = 2as to get the landing speed, v = √(2 × 9.81 × 45.0) = 29.7 m/s, then a = Δv ÷ Δt to get the time, t = 29.7 ÷ 9.81 = 3.03 s. Same answer, two different syllabuses.


## 7. The same cliff, but this time you run off it
`A-LEVEL` · Motion & acceleration · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

From the same 45 m cliff, a stone is thrown horizontally at 12 m/s. How long is it in the air, and how far from the foot of the cliff does it land?

**KNOWN** — height = 45.0 m  |  horizontal speed = 12.0 m/s  |  g = 9.81 m/s²

**WANTED** — the time of flight and the horizontal range

**STEPS**

1. **Split the motion into two problems that do not talk to each other**  
   Gravity acts vertically. It has no horizontal component, so it cannot change the horizontal speed – and the horizontal speed cannot change the fall.  
   `vertical: u = 0, a = 9.81 m/s²    |    horizontal: v = 12.0 m/s, a = 0`

2. **The vertical half is problem 6, word for word**  
   Same height, same g, same u = 0, so the same time.  
   `t = √(2 × 45.0 ÷ 9.81) = 3.029 s`

3. **The horizontal half has no acceleration**  
   Constant speed, so distance is just speed multiplied by time.  
   `range = 12.0 × 3.029 = 36.3 m`

**ANSWER — t = 3.03 s and range = 36.3 m**  
Thrown or dropped, the stone hits the water at the same instant.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer a time longer than the 3.03 s of problem 6**  
Assuming that more horizontal speed keeps the stone up for longer. It does not. The vertical motion knows nothing about the horizontal motion. Drop one stone and fire another sideways at the same moment from the same height, and they land together – the fast one simply lands further out.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Set the horizontal speed to zero and the problem should collapse back into problem 6. It does: t = 3.03 s either way.


## 8. Why you feel heavy when a lift sets off
`A-LEVEL` · Newton's Laws · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A 65 kg person stands on bathroom scales inside a lift. The lift accelerates upwards at 1.8 m/s². What reading, in newtons, do the scales show?

**KNOWN** — m = 65.0 kg  |  a = 1.8 m/s² upwards  |  g = 9.81 m/s²

**WANTED** — the normal contact force N from the scales

**STEPS**

1. **The scales read the contact force, not the weight**  
   A set of scales measures how hard you press on it, which by Newton’s third law is how hard it presses back on you.  
   `reading = N`

2. **Only two vertical forces act on the person**  
   Weight down, normal force up. The person is accelerating upwards, so the upward force has to be the larger one.  
   `W = mg = 65.0 × 9.81 = 637.6 N, downwards`

3. **Apply Newton’s second law, taking up as positive**  
   `N − mg = ma    →    N = m(g + a)`

4. **Substitute**  
   `N = 65.0 × (9.81 + 1.8) = 65.0 × 11.61 = 754.6 N`

**ANSWER — 755 N**  
Standing still the scales would read 638 N, so you appear 18% heavier.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 117 N**  
Writing N = ma and stopping there. That is the NET force, not the reading – the scales still have to hold the weight up as well. Leaving mg out gives an answer that is only 16% of the truth, which would mean the scales read LESS while accelerating upwards. That is the opposite of what anyone has ever felt in a lift.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Divide the reading by g to see it as a mass: 76.9 kg. The scales behave as though the person had gained about 12 kg, which is exactly the heavy feeling in the first second of the ride.


## 9. A crate on a ramp: does it slide?
`A-LEVEL` · Force · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A 4.0 kg crate sits on a ramp inclined at 25°. The coefficient of friction between crate and ramp is 0.30. Show whether the crate slides, and if it does, find its acceleration.

**KNOWN** — m = 4.0 kg  |  incline = 25 °  |  μ = 0.30  |  g = 9.81 m/s²

**WANTED** — whether the crate slides, and its acceleration if it does

**STEPS**

1. **Resolve the weight along the slope and into it**  
   Not horizontally and vertically. On an incline it is the slope that sets the useful pair of directions.  
   `along: mg sin 25° = 16.58 N     into: mg cos 25° = 35.56 N`

2. **The normal force balances only the part pressing in**  
   The ramp pushes back exactly as hard as it is pressed, and only at right angles to its surface.  
   `N = mg cos 25° = 35.56 N`

3. **Find the largest friction force available**  
   `F(max) = μN = 0.30 × 35.56 = 10.67 N`

4. **Compare the two**  
   The pull down the slope beats the most that friction can offer, so the crate moves.  
   `16.58 N > 10.67 N    →    it slides`

5. **Newton’s second law, along the slope**  
   `a = (16.58 − 10.67) ÷ 4.0 = 1.48 m/s²`

**ANSWER — it slides, at 1.48 m/s²**  
Down the slope, and only about 15% of free-fall acceleration.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer F(max) = 11.8 N**  
Using N = mg = 39.2 N. On a slope the surface never carries the whole weight, only the component pressing into it, so this overstates friction by 10%. The dangerous version of this mistake is on a shallow ramp: between about 16.7° and 17.5° the wrong method says the crate stays put when in fact it is already sliding.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
A crate slides whenever tan(angle) > μ. Here tan 25° = 0.466, which beats 0.30 – and notice the mass never appeared in that comparison at all.


## 10. The same crate on a gentler ramp – so how big is the friction now?
`A-LEVEL` · Force · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

The same 4.0 kg crate is placed on a ramp at 15°, where the coefficient of static friction is 0.40. The crate does not move. State the size of the friction force acting on it.

**KNOWN** — m = 4.0 kg  |  incline = 15 °  |  μs = 0.40  |  g = 9.81 m/s²

**WANTED** — the actual friction force on the crate

**STEPS**

1. **First check that it really does stay put**  
   `tan 15° = 0.268, which is less than μ = 0.40    →    it does not move`

2. **Not moving means in equilibrium**  
   Nothing is accelerating, so along the slope the forces must cancel exactly.  
   `friction = the component of weight down the slope`

3. **Resolve the weight**  
   `mg sin 15° = 4.0 × 9.81 × 0.2588 = 10.2 N`

4. **And that is the friction**  
   Friction supplies exactly what is needed to hold the crate, and not a newton more.  
   `F = 10.2 N, up the slope`

**ANSWER — 10.2 N**  
Up the slope, exactly balancing the pull of gravity along it.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 15.2 N**  
Working out μN and quoting it. But μN is the MAXIMUM static friction, not the actual one: the law is F ≤ μN, not F = μN. The crate needs only 10.2 N to stay still, so that is all friction supplies; the remaining 5.00 N is held in reserve. Quoting 15.2 N would leave a net force UP the slope, with the crate climbing it unaided.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The size of the reserve is what tells you the crate is stable, and comfortably so: friction is using only 67% of what it has available.


## 11. Dragging a suitcase through an airport
`A-LEVEL` · Work · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A suitcase is pulled 20 m across level floor by a handle held at 30° above the horizontal, with a force of 45 N along the handle. How much work does the pulling force do?

**KNOWN** — F = 45 N  |  d = 20 m  |  angle to the floor = 30 °

**WANTED** — the work done by the pulling force

**STEPS**

1. **Only the part of the force along the motion does work**  
   The suitcase travels horizontally. The upward part of the pull moves it nowhere, so it does no work at all.  
   `W = Fd cos θ`

2. **Resolve the force onto the direction of travel**  
   `F(horizontal) = 45 cos 30° = 45 × 0.8660 = 38.97 N`

3. **Multiply by the distance moved**  
   `W = 38.97 × 20 = 779.4 J`

**ANSWER — 779 J**  
About 780 joules over the twenty metres.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 900 J**  
Multiplying 45 × 20 and forgetting cos 30°, which assumes you are pulling horizontally. The vertical component of the pull is perfectly real – it is lifting weight off the wheels – but since the case never moves upwards, that component does zero work. Dropping the cosine inflates the answer by 15%.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
cos 30° is 0.8660, so the answer has to come out at roughly 87% of 900 J. 779 J does.


## 12. The slide, and why your weight makes no difference
`GCSE` · Energy · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A child starts from rest at the top of a smooth slide, 3.2 m above the ground. How fast are they moving at the bottom? The mass of the child is not given.

**KNOWN** — h = 3.2 m  |  u = 0 m/s  |  friction = none

**WANTED** — the speed at the bottom of the slide

**STEPS**

1. **Energy in equals energy out**  
   The slide is smooth, so nothing is lost to friction: every joule of gravitational potential energy becomes kinetic energy.  
   `mgh = ½mv²`

2. **The mass cancels**  
   Which is why the question is allowed to withhold it – and why withholding it is the test.  
   `gh = ½v²`

3. **Rearrange**  
   `v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 3.2) = √62.78`

4. **Solve**  
   `v = 7.92 m/s`

**ANSWER — 7.92 m/s**  
Independent of mass: a heavier child arrives at exactly the same speed.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “not enough information, the mass is missing”**  
The mass is missing because it cancels. Every energy-conversion problem of this shape loses it on the second line. When a quantity is absent from a question, the first thing to test is whether the algebra removes it – not whether the examiner has made a mistake.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
That is 28.5 km/h, which is quick for a playground – and it should be, because the question deleted friction. A real slide of that height delivers noticeably less.


## 13. Running up the stairs
`GCSE` · Energy · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A 58 kg student runs up a flight of stairs of total height 4.2 m in 6.5 s. Calculate the useful power they develop.

**KNOWN** — m = 58 kg  |  h = 4.2 m  |  t = 6.5 s

**WANTED** — the useful power developed

**STEPS**

1. **Find the work done against gravity**  
   Only the height counts. Walking along each tread does no work against gravity, because gravity has no horizontal component.  
   `W = mgh = 58 × 9.81 × 4.2 = 2390 J`

2. **Power is work per second**  
   `P = W ÷ t`

3. **Divide**  
   `P = 2390 ÷ 6.5 = 367.7 W`

**ANSWER — 368 W**  
About 0.37 kW, or roughly half a horsepower.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 2390 W**  
Quoting the work done and labelling it as power. Joules and watts are not interchangeable – a watt is a joule per second. The giveaway is that the time would then be left unused: if a question gives you a number and your answer never touches it, something has gone wrong.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
A fit person holds 150-250 W for a long time and several hundred watts in a short burst. 368 W sustained for 6.5 s is a hard sprint up the stairs, which is exactly what the question describes.


## 14. Two trolleys that stick together
`BOTH` · Momentum · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A 0.80 kg trolley moving at 3.0 m/s collides with a stationary 1.20 kg trolley. They stick together. Find their common velocity afterwards, and how much kinetic energy is lost.

**KNOWN** — mA = 0.80 kg  |  uA = 3.0 m/s  |  mB = 1.20 kg  |  uB = 0 m/s

**WANTED** — the velocity after the collision, and the kinetic energy lost

**STEPS**

1. **Momentum is conserved in every collision, without exception**  
   No external horizontal force acts on the pair, so the total momentum before equals the total after.  
   `p(before) = 0.80 × 3.0 + 1.20 × 0 = 2.40 kg m/s`

2. **Afterwards they move as a single object**  
   `p(after) = (0.80 + 1.20)v = 2.00v`

3. **Set them equal and solve**  
   `v = 2.40 ÷ 2.00 = 1.20 m/s`

4. **Now compare the kinetic energies**  
   Kinetic energy is not conserved here, and the second half of the question exists to make you prove it.  
   `KE(before) = ½ × 0.80 × 3.0² = 3.60 J`

5. **And afterwards**  
   `KE(after) = ½ × 2.00 × 1.20² = 1.44 J`

6. **The difference is what was lost**  
   It went into deforming the buffers, into sound, and into heat.  
   `lost = 3.60 − 1.44 = 2.16 J`

**ANSWER — v = 1.20 m/s, and 2.16 J lost**  
60.0% of the kinetic energy disappears, while the momentum does not change at all.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer v = 1.90 m/s**  
Conserving kinetic energy instead of momentum. Momentum is conserved in every collision; kinetic energy only in a perfectly elastic one, and two objects that stick together are the least elastic collision there is. Whenever a question says “stick”, “couple” or “embed”, kinetic energy is about to be lost.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The same momentum is now carried by 2.50 times the mass, so the speed has to fall by that same factor: 3.0 ÷ 2.50 = 1.20 m/s.


## 15. What an airbag actually does
`BOTH` · Momentum · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A 70 kg driver travelling at 14 m/s is brought to rest in a crash. Against the steering column this takes 0.040 s; against an airbag it takes 0.25 s. Find the average force in each case.

**KNOWN** — m = 70 kg  |  u = 14 m/s  |  v = 0 m/s  |  t without airbag = 0.040 s  |  t with airbag = 0.25 s

**WANTED** — the average force on the driver in each case

**STEPS**

1. **Find the change in momentum**  
   This is fixed by the crash itself, not by what the driver happens to hit.  
   `Δp = m(v − u) = 70 × (0 − 14) = −980 kg m/s`

2. **Force is the rate of change of momentum**  
   Newton’s second law in its original form, and the more useful one here.  
   `F = Δp ÷ Δt`

3. **Without the airbag**  
   `F = 980 ÷ 0.040 = 24 500 N`

4. **With the airbag**  
   The identical momentum change, spread over six times as long.  
   `F = 980 ÷ 0.25 = 3920 N`

**ANSWER — 24 500 N without the airbag, 3920 N with it**  
The airbag cuts the force by a factor of 6.25.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “the airbag reduces the momentum”**  
It does not, and it could not. The driver goes from 14 m/s to rest either way, so Δp is 980 kg m/s in both cases. The airbag changes only Δt. That is the whole of its engineering: it is not soft so that it absorbs the blow, it is deep so that the blow takes longer.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
As multiples of body weight: 35.7g against the column and 5.71g against the airbag. A rollercoaster peaks near 5g. The airbag does not make the crash gentle – it moves it from a number the body cannot survive to one it can.


## 16. How fast you can take a roundabout
`A-LEVEL` · Force · Part 2 · Mechanics (pp. 17-34)

**PROBLEM**

A car drives round a flat roundabout of radius 25 m. The coefficient of friction between tyres and road is 0.70. Find the maximum speed before it skids. The mass of the car is not given.

**KNOWN** — r = 25 m  |  μ = 0.70  |  g = 9.81 m/s²

**WANTED** — the maximum cornering speed

**STEPS**

1. **Ask what is providing the centripetal force**  
   Nothing pushes the car round the bend except friction between tyre and road. On a flat roundabout there is no other horizontal force available at all.  
   `friction = centripetal force`

2. **Write both sides out**  
   `μmg = mv² ÷ r`

3. **The mass cancels**  
   It sits on both sides, which is exactly why the question is entitled to leave it out.  
   `μg = v² ÷ r`

4. **Rearrange and solve**  
   `v = √(μgr) = √(0.70 × 9.81 × 25) = √171.7 = 13.1 m/s`

**ANSWER — 13.1 m/s**  
That is 47.2 km/h, or 29.3 mph.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “a heavier car has to go slower”**  
Mass cancels. A loaded lorry and an empty hatchback skid at the same speed on the same bend, because the extra mass needs more centripetal force but also presses the tyres down harder and earns precisely that much more friction. What does change the answer is μ – which is why the same roundabout is lethal in the wet.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
In the wet μ falls to roughly 0.40, giving 22.2 mph instead of 29.3 mph. That gap is the physics behind every “reduce speed in wet conditions” sign on the network.

## 17. The pressure on a diver at the bottom of the pool
`GCSE` · Pressure · Part 3 · Fluids (pp. 35-46)

**PROBLEM**

A diving pool is 3.8 m deep. Water has a density of 1000 kg/m³ and atmospheric pressure at the surface is 101 000 Pa. Find the TOTAL pressure on a diver at the bottom.

**KNOWN** — h = 3.8 m  |  ρ = 1000 kg/m³  |  atmospheric pressure = 101 000 Pa  |  g = 9.81 m/s²

**WANTED** — the total pressure at the bottom of the pool

**STEPS**

1. **Find the pressure the water alone adds**  
   Pressure in a liquid depends only on depth, density and g. The shape of the pool makes no difference whatsoever.  
   `P(water) = ρgh = 1000 × 9.81 × 3.8 = 37 278 Pa`

2. **The atmosphere is still sitting on top of it**  
   The water does not hold the air up. The whole column of atmosphere presses down on the pool surface and that pressure is passed on through the liquid.  
   `P(total) = P(atmospheric) + P(water)`

3. **Add them**  
   `P = 101 000 + 37 278 = 138 278 Pa`

**ANSWER — 138 kPa**  
About 1.37 times atmospheric pressure, at a depth you could stand up in.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 37 300 Pa**  
Giving only the water’s contribution. That answers a different question – the GAUGE pressure, which is what a pressure gauge reads because it has air on its other side. The question asked for the total, and the atmosphere is 73.0% of it. Read whether a question wants total (absolute) or gauge pressure before you write anything down.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Roughly 10 m of water is worth one atmosphere. At 3.8 m you would therefore expect about 0.37 extra atmospheres, and 37 278 ÷ 101 000 = 0.369. The depth rule and the equation agree.


## 18. Does it float, and does the size of it matter?
`GCSE` · Density · Part 3 · Fluids (pp. 35-46)

**PROBLEM**

A rectangular pine block measures 20 cm × 12 cm × 5.0 cm and has a mass of 0.68 kg. Water has a density of 1000 kg/m³. Show whether the block floats, and state what fraction of it sits below the waterline.

**KNOWN** — dimensions = 0.20 × 0.12 × 0.050 m  |  mass = 0.68 kg  |  ρ water = 1000 kg/m³

**WANTED** — whether it floats, and the fraction submerged

**STEPS**

1. **Work in metres from the start**  
   Mixing centimetres into an SI calculation is the single most common way to lose this mark.  
   `V = 0.20 × 0.12 × 0.050 = 0.0012 m³`

2. **Find the density**  
   `ρ = 0.68 ÷ 0.0012 = 567 kg/m³`

3. **Compare with the liquid, not with anything else**  
   Floating is decided by density against density. Mass on its own tells you nothing.  
   `567 < 1000    →    it floats`

4. **A floating object sinks until it displaces its own weight**  
   So the fraction submerged is just the ratio of the two densities.  
   `fraction = 567 ÷ 1000 = 0.567, or 56.7%`

**ANSWER — it floats, with 56.7% of it submerged**  
Just over half the block sits below the waterline.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “a bigger block of the same wood would sink”**  
It would not. Double every dimension and the mass goes up eight-fold – but so does the volume, so the density is unchanged and the block floats exactly as high in the water. Floating is a property of the material, not of the lump. A pine log and a pine matchstick float with the same fraction submerged.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Pine runs about 350-600 kg/m³ dry. 567 sits inside that band, so the number describes real timber – and it predicts the block floating a little over half under, which is what a plank in a pond actually does.


## 19. A trolley jack, and the thing it does not do
`BOTH` · Pascal's Principle · Part 3 · Fluids (pp. 35-46)

**PROBLEM**

In a hydraulic jack the small piston has an area of 2.0 cm² and the large piston 150 cm². A force of 90 N is applied to the small piston. Find the force on the large one, and the mass it could lift.

**KNOWN** — A small = 2.0 cm² = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m²  |  A large = 150 cm² = 150 × 10⁻⁴ m²  |  F small = 90 N

**WANTED** — the output force, and the mass it can lift

**STEPS**

1. **Pascal: the pressure is the same everywhere in the fluid**  
   This is the whole principle. The liquid cannot tell the two pistons apart; it only knows pressure.  
   `P = F ÷ A = 90 ÷ 0.00020 = 450 000 Pa`

2. **Apply that same pressure to the larger area**  
   `F = PA = 450 000 × 0.0150 = 6750 N`

3. **Turn the force into a mass**  
   `m = F ÷ g = 6750 ÷ 9.81 = 688 kg`

**ANSWER — 6750 N, enough to lift 688 kg**  
The jack multiplies the force by 75, which is exactly the ratio of the two areas.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “so the jack multiplies the energy by 75 as well”**  
It does not, and nothing could. Energy is conserved: to raise the large piston by 1 cm you must push the small one down 75 cm, because the same volume of oil has to move. Multiply force by 75 and divide distance by 75 and the work done is identical at both ends. A hydraulic jack trades distance for force – it does not create anything.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
688 kg is comfortably more than one corner of a family car, which is precisely the job a trolley jack is built for. And the force ratio came out at 75, matching the area ratio exactly, as Pascal requires.


## 20. Why a steel block weighs less underwater
`A-LEVEL` · Archimedes' Principle · Part 3 · Fluids (pp. 35-46)

**PROBLEM**

A steel block of volume 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m³ and density 7800 kg/m³ hangs from a spring balance, fully submerged in water. What does the balance read?

**KNOWN** — V = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m³  |  ρ steel = 7800 kg/m³  |  ρ water = 1000 kg/m³  |  g = 9.81 m/s²

**WANTED** — the apparent weight shown on the balance

**STEPS**

1. **Find the real weight first**  
   `W = ρVg = 7800 × 0.00025 × 9.81 = 19.13 N`

2. **Archimedes: upthrust equals the weight of fluid displaced**  
   Displaced – so the density in this line is the WATER’s, never the block’s.  
   `U = ρ(water) × V × g = 1000 × 0.00025 × 9.81 = 2.45 N`

3. **The balance reads what is left**  
   `reading = 19.13 − 2.45 = 16.68 N`

**ANSWER — 16.7 N**  
The block appears to lose 2.45 N, about 12.8% of its weight.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer a reading of 0 N**  
Using the block’s own density in the upthrust line instead of the water’s. That makes the upthrust equal the weight for every object ever submerged, so nothing would ever sink. The moment an answer implies that steel floats, the error is in which density went into the formula.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The fraction of weight lost should be ρ(water) ÷ ρ(steel) = 1000 ÷ 7800 = 0.128. The calculation gives 0.128. Two independent routes to the same figure.


## 21. Water through a narrowing pipe
`A-LEVEL` · Bernoulli's Principle · Part 3 · Fluids (pp. 35-46)

**PROBLEM**

Water flows at 1.2 m/s through a pipe of internal diameter 50 mm, which narrows to 25 mm. Find the speed in the narrow section, and say whether the pressure there is higher or lower, and by how much.

**KNOWN** — v1 = 1.2 m/s  |  d1 = 50 mm  |  d2 = 25 mm  |  ρ = 1000 kg/m³

**WANTED** — the speed in the narrow section and the pressure change

**STEPS**

1. **The same water per second has to pass every cross-section**  
   Nothing is being stored or lost along the pipe, so A1v1 = A2v2.  
   `A1v1 = A2v2`

2. **Area goes with the SQUARE of the diameter**  
   The diameter halves, so the area quarters – the step that decides the whole answer.  
   `A1 ÷ A2 = (50 ÷ 25)² = 4`

3. **So the speed goes up four-fold**  
   `v2 = 1.2 × 4 = 4.8 m/s`

4. **Bernoulli: faster flow means lower pressure**  
   The water speeds up, so something must have pushed it. That push is a pressure DROP from wide section to narrow.  
   `ΔP = ½ρ(v2² − v1²) = 500 × (23.04 − 1.44) = 10 800 Pa lower`

**ANSWER — 4.8 m/s, at a pressure 10 800 Pa LOWER**  
The narrow section is the fast section, and the fast section is the low-pressure one.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “the pressure is higher in the narrow bit, because the water is squeezed”**  
This is the most persistent wrong intuition in fluids, and it is backwards. Squeezing a static fluid does raise its pressure – but this fluid is not static. The water arrives at the constriction and SPEEDS UP, and the only thing that can accelerate it is a force, which means a push from high pressure behind towards low pressure ahead. The pressure has to fall for the water to go faster. It is why an aerofoil lifts and why two sheets of paper pull together when you blow between them.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
10 800 Pa is about 11% of atmospheric pressure, or the pressure under 1.1 m of water. Small, but easily enough to show on a manometer across the constriction – which is exactly how a Venturi flow meter measures flow rate.

## 22. How long the kettle really takes
`GCSE` · Heat energy · Part 4 · Thermodynamics (pp. 47-58)

**PROBLEM**

A kettle holds 1.5 kg of water at 18 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4180 J/kg°C and the kettle delivers 2.8 kW. How much energy is needed to bring it to 100 °C, and how long should that take?

**KNOWN** — m = 1.5 kg  |  c = 4180 J/kg°C  |  start = 18 °C  |  finish = 100 °C  |  P = 2800 W

**WANTED** — the energy required, and the time taken

**STEPS**

1. **ΔT is the CHANGE in temperature, not the final one**  
   The water does not start at zero. This one line is the whole problem.  
   `ΔT = 100 − 18 = 82 °C`

2. **Apply the specific heat capacity equation**  
   `Q = mcΔT = 1.5 × 4180 × 82 = 514 140 J`

3. **Power is energy per second, so time is energy over power**  
   `t = Q ÷ P = 514 140 ÷ 2800 = 184 s`

**ANSWER — 514 kJ, taking 184 s**  
Just over three minutes – which is how long a kettle actually takes.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 627 000 J**  
Using 100 as ΔT, because 100 is the number in the question. But the water begins at 18 °C, so only 82 degrees of heating are needed. Taking the final temperature instead of the rise overstates the energy by 22% – and it would predict a kettle that takes 224 s, which anyone who has made tea knows is wrong.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
A 3 kW kettle boils a litre and a half in roughly three minutes from cold tap water. The calculation gives 184 s. When a thermal answer can be checked against something in your own kitchen, check it.


## 23. Melting ice without warming it at all
`GCSE` · Heat energy · Part 4 · Thermodynamics (pp. 47-58)

**PROBLEM**

How much energy is needed to melt 0.25 kg of ice that is already at 0 °C, into water at 0 °C? The specific latent heat of fusion of water is 334 000 J/kg.

**KNOWN** — m = 0.25 kg  |  L(f) = 334 000 J/kg  |  start = ice at 0 °C  |  finish = water at 0 °C

**WANTED** — the energy needed to melt it

**STEPS**

1. **Notice that the temperature never changes**  
   It goes in at 0 °C and comes out at 0 °C. So mcΔT contributes nothing at all here – ΔT is zero.  
   `ΔT = 0, so Q = mcΔT = 0`

2. **All the energy goes into breaking the bonds, not into speeding molecules up**  
   Temperature measures the kinetic energy of the particles. During a change of state the energy goes into potential energy instead, pulling the lattice apart.  
   `Q = mL(f)`

3. **Substitute**  
   `Q = 0.25 × 334 000 = 83 500 J`

**ANSWER — 83.5 kJ**  
And not one degree of temperature rise to show for it.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “the ice warms up as it melts”**  
It does not. An ice-water mixture sits at 0 °C and stays there until the last of the ice has gone, no matter how much energy you pour in. That is why a drink with ice in it holds its temperature and a drink without it does not – and it is why the flat section of a heating curve is flat.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Put that 83.5 kJ into the resulting water instead and it would raise its temperature by 83 500 ÷ (0.25 × 4180) = 79.9 °C. Merely melting ice costs as much energy as heating the same water from freezing to nearly boiling.


## 24. Mixing hot and cold water
`BOTH` · Temperature · Part 4 · Thermodynamics (pp. 47-58)

**PROBLEM**

0.30 kg of water at 80 °C is mixed with 0.50 kg of water at 20 °C in an insulated container. Find the final temperature.

**KNOWN** — m hot = 0.30 kg at 80 °C  |  m cold = 0.50 kg at 20 °C  |  heat lost to surroundings = none

**WANTED** — the final temperature of the mixture

**STEPS**

1. **The energy the hot water loses, the cold water gains**  
   Nothing escapes the container, so the two must balance exactly.  
   `m(hot) c (80 − T) = m(cold) c (T − 20)`

2. **The specific heat capacity cancels**  
   It is the same liquid on both sides, so its value never has to be looked up.  
   `0.30(80 − T) = 0.50(T − 20)`

3. **Expand and collect**  
   `24 − 0.30T = 0.50T − 10    →    34.0 = 0.80T`

4. **Solve**  
   `T = 34.0 ÷ 0.80 = 42.5 °C`

**ANSWER — 42.5 °C**  
Below the midpoint, because there is more cold water than hot.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 50 °C**  
Averaging the two temperatures. That is only correct when the two masses are equal, and here they are not: there is 1.67 times as much cold water as hot, so the mixture has to settle nearer the cold end. The average is a special case, not the rule.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The answer must lie between 20 and 80 °C, and nearer 20 because the cold mass is larger. 42.5 °C satisfies both. Check it the other way: the hot water falls 37.5 °C and the cold rises 22.5 °C, and 0.30 × 37.5 = 0.50 × 22.5. Energy balances.


## 25. Why your tyre pressure is wrong after a motorway run
`A-LEVEL` · Gases · Part 4 · Thermodynamics (pp. 47-58)

**PROBLEM**

A car tyre is checked cold at 15 °C and holds air at an absolute pressure of 3.4 bar. After a long drive the air inside has reached 45 °C. The volume is effectively unchanged. Find the new pressure.

**KNOWN** — P1 = 3.4 bar absolute  |  T1 = 15 °C  |  T2 = 45 °C  |  V = constant

**WANTED** — the new absolute pressure

**STEPS**

1. **Convert both temperatures to kelvin, before anything else**  
   The gas laws are proportional relationships, and a proportion needs a scale that starts at true zero. Celsius does not; kelvin does.  
   `T1 = 15 + 273 = 288 K    T2 = 45 + 273 = 318 K`

2. **Constant volume, so pressure is proportional to temperature**  
   `P1 ÷ T1 = P2 ÷ T2`

3. **Rearrange and substitute**  
   `P2 = 3.4 × 318 ÷ 288 = 3.75 bar`

**ANSWER — 3.75 bar absolute**  
A rise of 0.35 bar, which is why tyres are always checked cold.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 10.2 bar**  
Using degrees Celsius, so that 45 °C looks like three times 15 °C and the pressure appears to triple. It does not: in kelvin the temperature rose from 288 to 318, a rise of only 10%. Celsius has an arbitrary zero, so ratios taken in it are meaningless. Any gas-law answer that changes by a factor of three for a warm afternoon is a units error.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The absolute temperature went up by 10%, so at constant volume the pressure must go up by 10% too: 3.4 × 1.104 = 3.75 bar. The percentage check and the equation agree, and both match the few tenths of a bar a garage gauge actually shows after a run.


## 26. A bubble on its way to the surface
`BOTH` · Gases · Part 4 · Thermodynamics (pp. 47-58)

**PROBLEM**

A bubble of volume 1.0 cm³ forms 12 m down in a lake and rises to the surface. The temperature does not change. Atmospheric pressure is 101 000 Pa and the water’s density is 1000 kg/m³. Find the bubble’s volume at the surface.

**KNOWN** — V1 = 1.0 cm³  |  depth = 12 m  |  atmospheric pressure = 101 000 Pa  |  temperature = constant

**WANTED** — the volume of the bubble at the surface

**STEPS**

1. **Find the ABSOLUTE pressure down at the bubble**  
   The water above it and the atmosphere above that both press on it. Boyle’s law needs the total, not just the water’s share.  
   `P1 = 101 000 + (1000 × 9.81 × 12) = 101 000 + 117 700 = 218 700 Pa`

2. **At the surface only the atmosphere is left**  
   `P2 = 101 000 Pa`

3. **Temperature is constant, so Boyle’s law applies**  
   `P1V1 = P2V2`

4. **Rearrange and solve**  
   `V2 = 1.0 × 218 700 ÷ 101 000 = 2.17 cm³`

**ANSWER — 2.17 cm³**  
The bubble more than doubles on the way up, without a single extra molecule entering it.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 1.17 cm³**  
Leaving the atmosphere out and using only the water’s 117 700 Pa. Boyle’s law is written in absolute pressures, and a bubble 12 m down is carrying the whole sky on its shoulders as well as the lake. Dropping the atmosphere here makes the bubble SHRINK as it rises, which is the opposite of what anyone has ever seen.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
About every 10 m of water adds one atmosphere. At 12 m the bubble sits under roughly 2.2 atmospheres, so at the surface it should be about 2.2 times bigger – and 2.17 cm³ is 2.17 times 1.0. It is also the reason divers are told never to hold their breath on the way up.


## 27. A power station that throws away more than it sells
`BOTH` · Entropy · Part 4 · Thermodynamics (pp. 47-58)

**PROBLEM**

A thermal power station burns fuel at a rate of 2400 MW and delivers 950 MW of electricity to the grid. Find its efficiency and the power it rejects as waste heat.

**KNOWN** — power in = 2400 MW  |  electrical power out = 950 MW

**WANTED** — the efficiency and the wasted power

**STEPS**

1. **Efficiency is useful output over total input**  
   Both are powers here, so no conversion to energy is needed – the seconds cancel.  
   `efficiency = 950 ÷ 2400 = 0.396`

2. **Express it as a percentage**  
   `= 39.6%`

3. **Everything that is not useful output leaves as heat**  
   Energy is conserved. It did not vanish; it went up the cooling towers and into the river.  
   `waste = 2400 − 950 = 1450 MW`

**ANSWER — 39.6% efficient, rejecting 1450 MW**  
The station discards more than half as much again as it sells.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “so 60.4% is being wasted through bad engineering”**  
Most of it is not an engineering failure at all – it is forbidden by the second law of thermodynamics. Any engine that turns heat into work must dump some heat at a lower temperature; that rejection is what makes the process possible, not a leak in it. Calling the whole of it waste misreads the physics.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Real coal and gas thermal stations run at about 38-42%. 39.6% sits right inside that band, which is the sign the numbers in the question describe a real plant rather than an invented one.

**ANOTHER WAY IN**  
A-level extension – how much of that loss is unavoidable. Carnot’s limit says the best possible efficiency is 1 − T(cold) ÷ T(hot), using absolute temperatures. With steam at 550 °C (823 K) and cooling water at 20 °C (293 K): 1 − 293 ÷ 823 = 0.644, or 64.4%. So the laws of physics cap this station at 64.4% and the engineers reached 39.6%. The gap worth criticising is that one – not the whole 60.4%.


## 28. What one window costs you overnight
`BOTH` · Heat energy · Part 4 · Thermodynamics (pp. 47-58)

**PROBLEM**

A window measures 1.4 m × 1.1 m and loses heat at a rate given by P = UAΔT, where U = 2.8 W/m²°C. Inside it is 21 °C and outside 4 °C. Find the rate of heat loss, and the energy lost in 24 hours.

**KNOWN** — dimensions = 1.4 × 1.1 m  |  U = 2.8 W/m²°C  |  inside = 21 °C  |  outside = 4 °C

**WANTED** — the rate of heat loss, and the energy lost in a day

**STEPS**

1. **Area, and the temperature DIFFERENCE**  
   As always in thermal problems, what drives the flow is the difference, not either temperature on its own.  
   `A = 1.4 × 1.1 = 1.54 m²    ΔT = 21 − 4 = 17 °C`

2. **Substitute into the equation given**  
   The answer is in watts, so it is a RATE – joules every second, continuously.  
   `P = 2.8 × 1.54 × 17 = 73.3 W`

3. **Energy is that rate multiplied by the time it runs for**  
   24 hours is 86 400 seconds. Convert before multiplying, not after.  
   `E = Pt = 73.30 × 86 400 = 6 333 120 J`

4. **And in the unit the energy bill uses**  
   `E = 73.3 W × 24 h = 1.76 kWh`

**ANSWER — 73.3 W, which is 6.33 MJ or 1.76 kWh per day**  
That one window leaks heat as steadily as a 73 W bulb left on, day and night.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer quoting 6 333 120 W, or 73.3 J**  
Swapping the two. A watt is a joule per second: the first answer is a rate and the second is a total, and they are not interchangeable labels for the same number. The giveaway is the time – a rate is true at every instant and never mentions a duration, while an energy is meaningless until you say over how long.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
1.76 kWh a day from one window. A house with ten similar windows loses around 17.6 kWh a day through the glazing alone, which is a serious share of a winter heating bill – and exactly why double glazing is sold on its U-value.

## 29. The one equation everything else is built on
`GCSE` · Resistance · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

A torch bulb has a potential difference of 3.0 V across it and a current of 0.25 A through it. Find its resistance, and the power it dissipates.

**KNOWN** — V = 3.0 V  |  I = 0.25 A

**WANTED** — the resistance of the bulb, and its power

**STEPS**

1. **Start from the definition, not from a memorised triangle**  
   Resistance is how much potential difference it takes to drive one amp through something. That sentence IS the equation.  
   `V = IR    →    R = V ÷ I`

2. **Substitute**  
   `R = 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = 12.0 Ω`

3. **Power is current times potential difference**  
   Volts are joules per coulomb and amps are coulombs per second, so multiplying them gives joules per second, which is watts.  
   `P = VI = 3.0 × 0.25 = 0.750 W`

**ANSWER — 12.0 Ω, dissipating 0.750 W**  
A small torch bulb, and three quarters of a watt of light and heat.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 0.0833 Ω**  
Dividing the wrong way round: I ÷ V instead of V ÷ I. The triangle is remembered upside down about as often as it is remembered correctly. The defence is not a better mnemonic, it is a sense of scale – 0.0833 Ω is the resistance of a short piece of copper wire, not of something that glows.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Check it the other way: 12.0 Ω carrying 0.25 A needs 12.0 × 0.25 = 3.00 V across it, which is what the question said. And torch bulbs do sit in the range of a few ohms to a few tens of ohms.


## 30. Two resistors in series
`GCSE` · Circuits · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

A 220 Ω resistor and a 330 Ω resistor are connected in series across a 12 V supply. Find the current, and the potential difference across each resistor.

**KNOWN** — R1 = 220 Ω  |  R2 = 330 Ω  |  V supply = 12 V

**WANTED** — the current, and the potential difference across each resistor

**STEPS**

1. **In series, resistances simply add**  
   There is only one path, so the charge has to fight its way through both. The obstacles come one after the other.  
   `R(total) = 220 + 330 = 550 Ω`

2. **One path means ONE current, the same everywhere**  
   Charge is not used up as it goes round. Whatever leaves the battery comes back to it.  
   `I = V ÷ R = 12 ÷ 550 = 0.02182 A`

3. **Now find each potential difference separately**  
   Each resistor gets the share of the voltage that its own resistance demands.  
   `V1 = 0.02182 × 220 = 4.80 V`

4. **And the second**  
   `V2 = 0.02182 × 330 = 7.20 V`

**ANSWER — 21.8 mA, with 4.80 V and 7.20 V across them**  
The larger resistor takes the larger share of the voltage, in exactly the same ratio as the resistances.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 6 V across each**  
Splitting the supply equally. The voltage divides in the ratio of the resistances, not evenly: 330 is 1.50 times 220, so it takes 1.50 times the voltage. Equal sharing only happens when the resistors are equal, which is a special case rather than the rule.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The two potential differences must add up to the supply: 4.80 + 7.20 = 12.00 V. They do. If they do not add to the supply voltage, something is wrong, and this check costs five seconds.


## 31. The same two resistors in parallel
`GCSE` · Circuits · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

The same 220 Ω and 330 Ω resistors are now connected in PARALLEL across the same 12 V supply. Find the total resistance and the total current.

**KNOWN** — R1 = 220 Ω  |  R2 = 330 Ω  |  V supply = 12 V

**WANTED** — the total resistance and the current drawn from the supply

**STEPS**

1. **In parallel it is the CONDUCTANCES that add, not the resistances**  
   You have not made the path harder, you have opened a second one. Two doors out of a room let more people through than one.  
   `1 ÷ R = 1 ÷ 220 + 1 ÷ 330`

2. **Put it over a common denominator**  
   `1 ÷ R = 3 ÷ 660 + 2 ÷ 660 = 5 ÷ 660`

3. **Invert – and this is the step people forget**  
   The equation gives you 1/R. It does not give you R until you turn it over.  
   `R = 660 ÷ 5 = 132 Ω`

4. **Now the total current**  
   `I = 12 ÷ 132 = 0.0909 A`

**ANSWER — 132 Ω, drawing 90.9 mA**  
Over four times the current the series version drew, from the same two components and the same battery.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 550 Ω, giving 21.8 mA**  
Adding the resistances, exactly as in series. This is the single most common mistake in school electricity, and it is 4.17 times out. It also fails the test that costs nothing: a parallel combination must ALWAYS come out smaller than the smallest resistor in it. Adding gives 550 Ω, which is larger than both. If your parallel answer is bigger than any single resistor, you have added when you should have inverted.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Two independent checks. First, 132 Ω is smaller than 220 Ω, as a parallel total must be. Second, add the branch currents: 12 ÷ 220 = 0.0546 A and 12 ÷ 330 = 0.0364 A, and 0.0546 + 0.0364 = 0.0909 A, matching the total. Both routes agree.


## 32. A potential divider, and which resistor goes on top
`A-LEVEL` · Potential · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

A 10 kΩ resistor and a 4.7 kΩ resistor are connected in series across a 9.0 V supply. Find the potential difference across the 4.7 kΩ resistor.

**KNOWN** — R1 = 10 kΩ  |  R2 = 4.7 kΩ  |  V supply = 9.0 V

**WANTED** — the potential difference across the 4.7 kΩ resistor

**STEPS**

1. **The two resistors share the supply in proportion to their resistance**  
   Same current through both, so V = IR means the voltages are in the same ratio as the resistances.  
   `V2 = V × R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)`

2. **The resistor you are asked about goes on TOP of the fraction**  
   This is the only thing that ever goes wrong with a potential divider. The denominator is always the total.  
   `V2 = 9.0 × 4700 ÷ 14 700`

3. **Solve**  
   `V2 = 2.88 V`

**ANSWER — 2.88 V**  
Leaving 6.12 V across the 10 kΩ.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 6.12 V**  
Putting the OTHER resistor on top: 9.0 × 10 000 ÷ 14 700. That is the voltage across the 10 kΩ, which is a perfectly good answer to a question nobody asked. Both numbers will appear on your calculator and both look plausible, which is precisely why this one is worth a check.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The 4.7 kΩ is the SMALLER resistor, so it must take LESS than half the supply – less than 4.5 V. 2.88 V passes; 6.12 V would have failed instantly. And the two shares add back to the supply: 2.88 + 6.12 = 9.00 V.


## 33. Three equations for power, and picking the one you can actually use
`BOTH` · Circuits · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

A 56 Ω resistor carries a current of 0.35 A. Calculate the power it dissipates. The supply voltage is not given.

**KNOWN** — R = 56 Ω  |  I = 0.35 A

**WANTED** — the power dissipated in the resistor

**STEPS**

1. **Write down all three versions before choosing**  
   They are the same equation wearing different clothes, because V = IR lets you swap between them.  
   `P = VI    P = I²R    P = V² ÷ R`

2. **Choose the one that uses only what you have**  
   You were given I and R, so the middle form needs nothing else. The other two would require a voltage you have not been told.  
   `P = I²R`

3. **Substitute, and square the current before multiplying**  
   `P = 0.35² × 56 = 0.1225 × 56 = 6.86 W`

**ANSWER — 6.86 W**  
Nearly seven watts, in a component the size of a grain of rice.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer reaching for P = VI and using the supply voltage**  
The V in every one of these equations is the potential difference across THAT component, never the voltage of the battery driving the circuit. In a circuit with anything else in it, those two are different numbers, and using the supply voltage silently credits one resistor with the power of the whole circuit.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Work out the missing voltage and check with a different equation: V = IR = 0.35 × 56 = 19.6 V, so P = VI = 19.6 × 0.35 = 6.86 W. The two forms agree. Worth noting too that 6.86 W would destroy a standard 0.25 W resistor, which is why power ratings exist.


## 34. What a tumble dryer really costs you in energy
`GCSE` · Current · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

A tumble dryer is rated at 2.2 kW and runs for 1.5 hours per cycle, four cycles a week. Find the energy used per cycle in kilowatt-hours and in joules, and the energy used in a year.

**KNOWN** — P = 2.2 kW  |  t per cycle = 1.5 h  |  cycles = 4 per week

**WANTED** — the energy per cycle, in kWh and in joules, and the energy per year

**STEPS**

1. **A kilowatt-hour is not a unit of power, it is a unit of energy**  
   It is what you get when a kilowatt runs for an hour. Power multiplied by time is always energy, whatever units you use.  
   `E = Pt = 2.2 × 1.5 = 3.3 kWh per cycle`

2. **Convert to joules**  
   One kWh is 1000 W running for 3600 s, which is 3.6 million joules.  
   `E = 3.3 × 3 600 000 = 11 880 000 J`

3. **Scale up to a year**  
   `E = 3.3 × 4 × 52 = 686.4 kWh per year`

**ANSWER — 3.3 kWh (11.9 MJ) per cycle, 686 kWh a year**  
From one appliance, used four times a week.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 2.2 kWh per cycle**  
Reading the rating off the label and calling it the energy. The 2.2 kW is a RATE – it tells you nothing until you say for how long. A dryer run for three hours uses twice the energy of one run for ninety minutes, while its rating never changes. Watts describe the appliance; kilowatt-hours describe what you did with it.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
A typical UK home uses roughly 2700 kWh of electricity a year. This one appliance accounts for about 25% of that, which is why tumble dryers are the first thing any energy-saving guide tells you to use less.


## 35. Counting the electrons
`BOTH` · Charge · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

A current of 0.80 A flows for 2.5 minutes. Find the charge that passes, and the number of electrons it corresponds to. The charge on an electron is 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

**KNOWN** — I = 0.80 A  |  t = 2.5 minutes  |  e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

**WANTED** — the charge transferred, and the number of electrons

**STEPS**

1. **Convert the time to seconds first**  
   An amp is defined as a coulomb per SECOND. Minutes have no place inside the equation.  
   `t = 2.5 × 60 = 150 s`

2. **Charge is current multiplied by time**  
   `Q = It = 0.80 × 150 = 120 C`

3. **Divide the total charge by the charge on one electron**  
   `n = 120 ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 7.50 × 10²⁰ electrons`

**ANSWER — 120 C, carried by 7.50 × 10²⁰ electrons**  
Two and a half minutes of a fairly ordinary current.

Leaving the time in minutes. The factor of 60 is the whole mistake, and the answer comes out sixty times too small. Any equation containing an amp, a watt or a volt is written in seconds, because all three are defined per second.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
7.50 × 10²⁰ sounds impossibly large until you compare it with a mole, 6.02 × 10²³. It is about a thousandth of a mole of electrons – a sliver of the charge carriers already sitting in the wire, drifting past very slowly indeed.


## 36. Choosing a fuse, and what a fuse is actually protecting
`GCSE` · Current · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

UK mains is 230 V. A kettle is rated at 2800 W and a reading lamp at 60 W. Fuses are available in 3 A, 5 A and 13 A. Choose the correct fuse for each, and say what the fuse is protecting.

**KNOWN** — V = 230 V  |  P kettle = 2800 W  |  P lamp = 60 W  |  fuses available = 3, 5, 13 A

**WANTED** — the right fuse for each appliance, and what the fuse protects

**STEPS**

1. **Find the normal working current of each**  
   `kettle: I = P ÷ V = 2800 ÷ 230 = 12.2 A`

2. **And the lamp**  
   `lamp: I = 60 ÷ 230 = 0.261 A`

3. **Pick the smallest fuse ABOVE the working current**  
   Above, so that it does not blow in normal use. The smallest such, so that it reacts as soon as anything goes wrong.  
   `kettle 12.2 A → 13 A     lamp 0.261 A → 3 A`

**ANSWER — 13 A for the kettle, 3 A for the lamp**  
And what the fuse protects is the flex, not the appliance.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 13 A for both, on the grounds that a bigger fuse is safer**  
It is the opposite. A fuse exists to melt before the CABLE overheats, so a 13 A fuse in a lamp flex lets 50 times the normal current flow before it acts – and a thin flex can be glowing long before that. Oversizing a fuse does not make a circuit safer, it makes the fuse ornamental.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Check the kettle from the other direction: a 13 A fuse at 230 V allows up to 2990 W, comfortably above the kettle’s 2800 W, while a 5 A fuse would allow only 1150 W and blow the moment it was switched on.


## 37. The force between two charges
`A-LEVEL` · Electric field · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

A charge of +2.5 µC and a charge of −4.0 µC are held 8.0 cm apart in air. Find the size and nature of the force between them. Take k = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻².

**KNOWN** — q1 = +2.5 µC = +2.5 × 10⁻⁶ C  |  q2 = −4.0 µC = −4.0 × 10⁻⁶ C  |  r = 8.0 cm = 0.080 m

**WANTED** — the magnitude of the force, and whether it attracts or repels

**STEPS**

1. **Convert everything to SI before touching the equation**  
   Microcoulombs and centimetres both have to go. The centimetre is the dangerous one, because it is squared.  
   `q1q2 = (2.5 × 10⁻⁶)(4.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.0 × 10⁻¹¹ C²`

2. **Square the separation**  
   `r² = 0.080² = 0.0064 m²`

3. **Apply Coulomb’s law**  
   Work with the magnitudes and decide attraction or repulsion from the signs afterwards. It is cleaner than carrying a minus through.  
   `F = kq1q2 ÷ r² = (8.99 × 10⁹ × 1.0 × 10⁻¹¹) ÷ 0.0064 = 14.0 N`

4. **Opposite signs attract**  
   `14.0 N, attractive`

**ANSWER — 14.0 N, attractive**  
Fourteen newtons between two specks of charge, at the width of a hand.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 1.12 N**  
Dividing by r instead of r². Coulomb’s law is an inverse SQUARE law, like gravity and like light, and forgetting the square here makes the force 12.5 times too small. If an answer to an inverse-square problem does not change by a factor of four when you double the distance, the square is missing.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Apply that very test: at 16 cm the force should be a quarter, 3.50 N. It is. And the sign check is independent of the arithmetic – one charge is positive and one negative, so whatever the size, the force has to pull them together.


## 38. Field and potential are not the same thing
`A-LEVEL` · Electric field · Part 5 · Electricity (pp. 59-72)

**PROBLEM**

Two parallel plates 2.5 cm apart have a potential difference of 300 V between them. Find the electric field strength between the plates, and the work done in moving a charge of 5.0 nC from one plate to the other.

**KNOWN** — V = 300 V  |  d = 2.5 cm = 0.025 m  |  Q = 5.0 nC = 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ C

**WANTED** — the electric field strength, and the work done moving the charge

**STEPS**

1. **Field strength is potential difference PER METRE**  
   That per metre is the whole distinction. Potential is measured at a place; field is how fast potential changes as you move.  
   `E = V ÷ d = 300 ÷ 0.025 = 12 000 V/m`

2. **Work done is charge multiplied by potential difference**  
   A volt is a joule per coulomb, so coulombs times volts gives joules. The units do the thinking for you.  
   `W = QV = (5.0 × 10⁻⁹) × 300`

3. **Solve**  
   `W = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ J = 1.5 µJ`

**ANSWER — E = 12 000 V/m and W = 1.5 µJ**  
A field of twelve thousand volts per metre, from a potential difference of only three hundred volts.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer E = 300 V/m**  
Quoting the potential difference and attaching the units of field strength. They are different quantities measured in different units: volts against volts per metre. The plates here are only 2.5 cm apart, so the field is 40.0 times the number on the supply. Squeeze the same 300 V into a tenth of the gap and the field is ten times larger again, which is exactly how a spark gap is designed.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Do the work a second way, through the field: W = QEd = (5.0 × 10⁻⁹) × 12 000 × 0.025 = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ J. The same answer by a different route, which is what you want whenever a problem offers two equations for the same quantity.

## 39. How long a radio wave actually is
`GCSE` · Waves · Part 6 · Waves & Optics (pp. 73-84)

**PROBLEM**

A radio station broadcasts on 93.5 MHz. Radio waves travel at 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. Find the wavelength.

**KNOWN** — f = 93.5 MHz  |  v = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s

**WANTED** — the wavelength of the broadcast

**STEPS**

1. **Convert the frequency to hertz first**  
   Mega means a million. The prefix is not decoration, it is a factor of 10⁶.  
   `f = 93.5 × 10⁶ Hz`

2. **Use the wave equation**  
   True of every wave there is, from ripples to gamma rays.  
   `v = fλ    →    λ = v ÷ f`

3. **Substitute**  
   `λ = (3.00 × 10⁸) ÷ (93.5 × 10⁶) = 3.21 m`

**ANSWER — 3.21 m**  
Just over three metres, from crest to crest.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 3.21 × 10⁶ m**  
Leaving the frequency in megahertz. The answer comes out a million times too long – a single wavelength stretching further than the British Isles. Any prefix in a question (M, k, m, µ, n) has to be turned into a power of ten before it goes anywhere near an equation.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
FM wavelengths are a few metres, and a quarter-wave aerial is a quarter of that: 0.80 m. That is almost exactly the length of the aerial on a car roof, which is not a coincidence – it is the design.


## 40. Reading a frequency off an oscilloscope
`GCSE` · Waves · Part 6 · Waves & Optics (pp. 73-84)

**PROBLEM**

An oscilloscope is set to 2.0 ms per division. One complete cycle of a sound wave spans 4.0 divisions across the screen. Find the period and the frequency.

**KNOWN** — timebase = 2.0 ms per division  |  one full cycle = 4.0 divisions

**WANTED** — the period and the frequency of the wave

**STEPS**

1. **Count the divisions for ONE complete cycle**  
   Crest to crest, or trough to trough. Crest to the next trough is only half a wave, and that is where this question is won or lost.  
   `4.0 divisions per cycle`

2. **Multiply by the timebase to get the period**  
   `T = 4.0 × 2.0 = 8.0 ms = 0.0080 s`

3. **Frequency is one over the period**  
   Period is seconds per cycle; frequency is cycles per second. They are the same information upside down.  
   `f = 1 ÷ T = 1 ÷ 0.0080 = 125 Hz`

**ANSWER — T = 8.0 ms and f = 125 Hz**  
A low note, well inside the range of human hearing.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 250 Hz**  
Measuring from a crest to the next trough and calling it a cycle. That is half a wave, so the period comes out halved and the frequency doubled. On a screen the two look equally like a complete shape, which is why the rule is to measure from one point to the next point that is identical in BOTH height and direction of travel.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
125 Hz is a note just below the open B string of a guitar. If the oscilloscope is showing a sound you can hear, the answer has to land between about 20 Hz and 20 000 Hz, and this does.


## 41. Shouting at a cliff
`GCSE` · Sound · Part 6 · Waves & Optics (pp. 73-84)

**PROBLEM**

A walker shouts towards a cliff face and hears the echo 1.6 s later. The speed of sound in air is 340 m/s. How far away is the cliff?

**KNOWN** — t = 1.6 s  |  v = 340 m/s

**WANTED** — the distance to the cliff face

**STEPS**

1. **Work out how far the SOUND travelled**  
   Not how far away the cliff is. Those are two different distances, and the question only hands you one of them directly.  
   `d = vt = 340 × 1.6 = 544 m`

2. **The sound made the journey twice**  
   Out to the cliff and back to the ear. That is what an echo is.  
   `distance to cliff = 544 ÷ 2`

3. **Halve it**  
   `= 272 m`

**ANSWER — 272 m**  
The cliff is less than three hundred metres away, though the sound covered twice that.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 544 m**  
Forgetting the return journey. It is the commonest error in every echo, sonar and radar question there is, and the factor is always exactly two. If a question involves something bouncing back, write 2d = vt before you write anything else.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
A rough field rule: sound covers about 170 m towards an object for every second of echo delay. 1.6 s × 170 = 272 m, which agrees with the calculation and gives you a way to check it without a calculator.


## 42. Light entering a glass block
`BOTH` · Refraction · Part 6 · Waves & Optics (pp. 73-84)

**PROBLEM**

A ray of light travelling through air strikes a glass block at 40° to the normal. The refractive index of the glass is 1.50. Find the angle of refraction inside the glass.

**KNOWN** — angle of incidence = 40 ° to the normal  |  n glass = 1.50  |  n air = 1.00

**WANTED** — the angle of refraction inside the glass

**STEPS**

1. **Every angle in optics is measured from the NORMAL**  
   The normal is the line at right angles to the surface. Not from the surface itself – that is the single most expensive habit in this topic.  
   `angle of incidence = 40° from the normal`

2. **Apply Snell’s law**  
   `n = sin i ÷ sin r    →    sin r = sin i ÷ n`

3. **Substitute**  
   `sin r = sin 40° ÷ 1.50 = 0.6428 ÷ 1.50 = 0.4285`

4. **Take the inverse sine**  
   `r = sin⁻¹ 0.4285 = 25.4°`

**ANSWER — 25.4 °**  
Measured from the normal, inside the glass.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 30.7°**  
Reading the angle from the surface instead of the normal, so that 40° becomes 50°. The two always add to 90°, which means the wrong value is right there on the diagram looking just as plausible. Draw the normal in, as a dashed line, before you measure anything.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Light entering a denser medium must bend TOWARDS the normal, so the answer has to be smaller than 40°. 25.4° is. Had the answer come out larger, the two sides of Snell’s law would have been the wrong way up.


## 43. The angle beyond which light cannot get out
`BOTH` · Reflection · Part 6 · Waves & Optics (pp. 73-84)

**PROBLEM**

The same glass has a refractive index of 1.50. Find its critical angle, and say what happens to a ray inside the glass that meets the surface at 45° to the normal.

**KNOWN** — n glass = 1.50  |  n air = 1.00  |  angle inside the glass = 45 ° to the normal

**WANTED** — the critical angle, and the fate of the 45 degree ray

**STEPS**

1. **The critical angle is the one that refracts to exactly 90 degrees**  
   At that angle the escaping ray skims along the surface. Any steeper and there is nowhere left for it to go.  
   `sin C = n(outside) ÷ n(inside) = 1.00 ÷ 1.50`

2. **Solve**  
   `C = sin⁻¹ 0.6667 = 41.8°`

3. **Compare the ray with the critical angle**  
   `45° > 41.8°    →    total internal reflection`

**ANSWER — C = 41.8°, and the 45° ray does not escape at all**  
It reflects back inside, as completely as from a mirror.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “it still gets out, just bent a long way”**  
Past the critical angle no light emerges – none, not a dim beam at a steep angle. The refracted ray would have to bend beyond 90°, which would mean going back into the glass it came from. The surface stops behaving like a window and starts behaving like a perfect mirror, which is exactly what makes an optical fibre work.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Run the same calculation for diamond, n = 2.42, and the critical angle falls to 24.4°. A much smaller critical angle means light entering a diamond is trapped and bounced around far more before it finds a way out – which is the physics of the sparkle.


## 44. Where a lens puts the image
`A-LEVEL` · Light · Part 6 · Waves & Optics (pp. 73-84)

**PROBLEM**

An object is placed 25 cm from a converging lens of focal length 15 cm. Find the position of the image and its magnification, and describe it.

**KNOWN** — u = 25 cm  |  f = 15 cm  |  lens = converging

**WANTED** — the image distance, the magnification, and the nature of the image

**STEPS**

1. **Write the lens equation and rearrange for the unknown**  
   `1 ÷ f = 1 ÷ u + 1 ÷ v    →    1 ÷ v = 1 ÷ f − 1 ÷ u`

2. **Substitute**  
   Keep both distances in the same unit and the answer comes out in that unit.  
   `1 ÷ v = 1 ÷ 15 − 1 ÷ 25 = 0.02667`

3. **Now INVERT, which is the step that gets forgotten**  
   The equation gives you one over v. It has not given you v.  
   `v = 1 ÷ 0.02667 = 37.5 cm`

4. **Magnification is image distance over object distance**  
   `m = v ÷ u = 37.5 ÷ 25 = 1.5`

**ANSWER — v = 37.5 cm, m = 1.5**  
A real, inverted image, half as big again as the object, on the far side of the lens.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 0.02667 cm**  
Stopping at 1/v and writing it down as the answer. An image distance of 0.02667 cm would put the image inside the glass of the lens itself. The units are the warning: 1/v comes out in reciprocal centimetres, and no distance was ever measured in those.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The object sits between f and 2f, that is between 15 cm and 30 cm. The standard result for that case is an image beyond 2f, real, inverted and magnified. The calculation puts it at 37.5 cm, which is beyond 30 cm, with a magnification above 1. Every part of the prediction matches.


## 45. The ambulance going past
`A-LEVEL` · Sound · Part 6 · Waves & Optics (pp. 73-84)

**PROBLEM**

An ambulance sounds a 850 Hz siren and drives past a stationary observer at 18 m/s. The speed of sound is 340 m/s. Find the frequency heard as it approaches and as it recedes.

**KNOWN** — f source = 850 Hz  |  v source = 18 m/s  |  v sound = 340 m/s

**WANTED** — the frequency heard approaching, and receding

**STEPS**

1. **The siren still emits 850 waves every second**  
   Nothing about the source changes. What changes is where each wave is emitted from, because the ambulance has moved on between one crest and the next.  
   `f(source) = 850 Hz throughout`

2. **Approaching, the crests are squeezed into a shorter distance**  
   Each new crest sets off from closer to you than the last, so the wavelength arriving is shorter and the frequency higher.  
   `f = f(source) × v ÷ (v − v(source))`

3. **Substitute for the approach**  
   `f = 850 × 340 ÷ 322 = 897.5 Hz`

4. **Receding, the sign flips**  
   `f = 850 × 340 ÷ 358 = 807.3 Hz`

**ANSWER — 898 Hz approaching, 807 Hz receding**  
A drop of 90.2 Hz the instant it passes you.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “the sound travels faster towards you”**  
It does not. The speed of sound is set by the air, not by whatever made the sound, and it is 340 m/s in every direction regardless of how fast the ambulance is moving. What the motion changes is the WAVELENGTH: the crests pile up ahead of the vehicle and spread out behind it. Same speed, different spacing, different pitch.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The pitch falls from 898 Hz to 807 Hz, a ratio of 1.112. On a musical scale that is close to a whole tone – and a whole tone is about what you hear an ambulance drop as it goes past.

## 46. Why a brighter light does not help
`A-LEVEL` · Quantum physics · Part 7 · Modern Physics (pp. 85-94)

**PROBLEM**

Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on a sodium surface whose work function is 2.3 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons. Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

**KNOWN** — λ = 400 nm = 400 × 10⁻⁹ m  |  work function = 2.3 eV  |  h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s

**WANTED** — the maximum kinetic energy of an emitted electron

**STEPS**

1. **Find the energy of ONE photon**  
   Light arrives in lumps. The whole of the photoelectric effect follows from that one sentence.  
   `E = hc ÷ λ = (1.989 × 10⁻²⁵) ÷ (4.00 × 10⁻⁷) = 4.973 × 10⁻¹⁹ J`

2. **Convert to electronvolts, because the work function is given in them**  
   `E = (4.973 × 10⁻¹⁹) ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 3.11 eV`

3. **The work function is the toll at the door**  
   Some energy is spent simply getting the electron out of the metal. Whatever is left is kinetic energy.  
   `KE(max) = E − ϕ = 3.11 − 2.3 = 0.81 eV`

**ANSWER — 0.81 eV, which is 1.30 × 10⁻¹⁹ J**  
And that is the MAXIMUM. Most electrons come out with less.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “turn the light up and the electrons come out faster”**  
They do not. Brightness is the number of photons per second, not the energy of each one. A brighter lamp of the same colour ejects MORE electrons at exactly the same maximum energy. To give each electron more energy you have to change the colour, not the intensity – and that fact is what killed the wave-only picture of light.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Find the threshold: a 2.3 eV work function needs a photon of at least that energy, so λ = hc ÷ ϕ = 5.40 × 10⁻⁷ m, about 540 nm, which is green. So this metal emits under blue and violet light and does nothing at all under red, however bright the red is.


## 47. Why a bright green laser is safer than a faint X-ray
`A-LEVEL` · Quantum physics · Part 7 · Modern Physics (pp. 85-94)

**PROBLEM**

Compare the energy of a photon of green light (λ = 550 nm) with a photon of X-radiation (λ = 0.10 nm). The ionisation energy of a hydrogen atom is 13.6 eV. Which of the two can ionise an atom?

**KNOWN** — green light = 550 nm  |  X-ray = 0.10 nm  |  ionisation energy of hydrogen = 13.6 eV

**WANTED** — the two photon energies, and which can ionise

**STEPS**

1. **Photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength**  
   Short wavelength means high energy. That one relationship orders the whole electromagnetic spectrum by how dangerous it is.  
   `E = hc ÷ λ`

2. **The green photon**  
   `E = (1.989 × 10⁻²⁵) ÷ (5.50 × 10⁻⁷) ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 2.26 eV`

3. **The X-ray photon**  
   `E = (1.989 × 10⁻²⁵) ÷ (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰) ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 12.43 keV`

4. **Compare each with 13.6 eV**  
   `2.26 eV < 13.6 eV     12 430 eV > 13.6 eV`

**ANSWER — green 2.26 eV, X-ray 12.4 keV – only the X-ray ionises**  
The X-ray photon carries about 5.5 thousand times the energy of the green one.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer “a powerful laser must be more dangerous than a weak X-ray source”**  
Ionisation happens one photon at a time, so what matters is the energy of the individual photon, not how many arrive. A hundred-watt green laser delivers an enormous number of photons, every one of them too feeble to ionise anything – it burns, but it cannot break a molecule apart. A single X-ray photon can.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
This is why a reading lamp is harmless and a dental X-ray is measured and shielded. It also explains sunburn: visible light at 2.26 eV does nothing to DNA, while ultraviolet, just beyond the violet end, finally crosses the threshold where chemical bonds start to break.


## 48. A radioactive source, a day later
`GCSE` · Radiation · Part 7 · Modern Physics (pp. 85-94)

**PROBLEM**

A radioactive source has an activity of 7200 Bq and a half-life of 6.0 hours. Find its activity 24 hours later.

**KNOWN** — initial activity = 7200 Bq  |  half-life = 6.0 hours  |  time elapsed = 24 hours

**WANTED** — the activity after 24 hours

**STEPS**

1. **Count how many half-lives have passed**  
   `n = 24 ÷ 6.0 = 4 half-lives`

2. **Each half-life halves the activity, so they compound**  
   Four halvings is not a quarter and it is not a half four times over. It is a half multiplied by itself four times.  
   `A = 7200 ÷ 2⁴ = 7200 ÷ 16`

3. **Solve**  
   `A = 450 Bq`

**ANSWER — 450 Bq**  
A sixteenth of what it started at, after four half-lives.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 1800 Bq**  
Dividing by the number of half-lives instead of halving that many times: 7200 ÷ 4 rather than 7200 ÷ 2⁴. Radioactive decay is exponential, not linear. The linear version also predicts that after eight half-lives the activity is negative, which should be enough to retire the method permanently.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
After four half-lives the fraction left is 1/16, which is 6.25% – and 6.25% of 7200 is 450 Bq. Note too that it never reaches zero: halving something repeatedly gets you arbitrarily close and never quite there.


## 49. The mass that goes missing when a nucleus forms
`A-LEVEL` · The atom · Part 7 · Modern Physics (pp. 85-94)

**PROBLEM**

A proton has a mass of 1.007276 u and a neutron 1.008665 u, yet a deuterium nucleus made of one of each has a mass of only 2.013553 u. Find the energy equivalent of the missing mass, in MeV. Take 1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.

**KNOWN** — proton = 1.007276 u  |  neutron = 1.008665 u  |  deuterium nucleus = 2.013553 u  |  1 u = 1.661 × 10⁻²⁷ kg

**WANTED** — the binding energy of the nucleus, in MeV

**STEPS**

1. **Find the mass that is unaccounted for**  
   The parts weigh more apart than they do together. That difference is not an error in the measurement; it is the point of the question.  
   `Δm = (1.007276 + 1.008665) − 2.013553 = 0.002388 u`

2. **Convert to kilograms**  
   `Δm = 0.002388 × (1.661 × 10⁻²⁷) = 3.966 × 10⁻³⁰ kg`

3. **Apply E = mc², and square c**  
   `E = (3.966 × 10⁻³⁰) × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 3.570 × 10⁻¹³ J`

4. **Convert to MeV**  
   `E = (3.570 × 10⁻¹³) ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 2.23 MeV`

**ANSWER — 2.23 MeV**  
The energy you would have to supply to pull the two particles apart again.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer 1.19 × 10⁻²¹ J**  
Multiplying by c instead of c². The square is the whole reason nuclear energies are millions of times chemical ones: it turns a factor of 3.00 × 10⁸ into 9.00 × 10¹⁶. Leaving it out makes the answer 3.00 × 10⁸ times too small.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
The accepted binding energy of the deuteron is 2.224 MeV. This calculation gives 2.231 MeV from nothing but three masses and a constant – agreement to within a fraction of a percent, which is the strongest possible sign the method is sound.


## 50. An electron with a wavelength
`A-LEVEL` · Quantum physics · Part 7 · Modern Physics (pp. 85-94)

**PROBLEM**

An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 100 V. Find its de Broglie wavelength. Take the mass of an electron as 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.

**KNOWN** — V = 100 V  |  m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg  |  e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C  |  h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s

**WANTED** — the de Broglie wavelength of the electron

**STEPS**

1. **The work done by the field becomes kinetic energy**  
   Charge multiplied by potential difference is energy, and the electron starts from rest, so all of it is kinetic.  
   `KE = eV = (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) × 100 = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ J`

2. **Get the speed from the kinetic energy**  
   `v = √(2KE ÷ m) = √(2 × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁷) ÷ (9.11 × 10⁻³¹)) = 5.927 × 10⁶ m/s`

3. **Momentum, not speed, is what goes into the de Broglie relation**  
   `p = mv = (9.11 × 10⁻³¹) × (5.927 × 10⁶) = 5.399 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m/s`

4. **Apply de Broglie**  
   `λ = h ÷ p = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) ÷ (5.399 × 10⁻²⁴) = 1.23 × 10⁻¹⁰ m`

**ANSWER — 1.23 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, or 0.123 nm**  
A particle with a wavelength about the width of an atom.

**⚠ THE TRAP — most students answer using λ = h ÷ v**  
Dividing by the speed instead of the momentum. The de Broglie relation pairs a wavelength with a MOMENTUM, which is why a cricket ball and an electron at the same speed have utterly different wavelengths – the mass is doing the work. Dropping the mass also leaves the units wrong, and the units are the fastest way to catch it.

**DOES IT MAKE SENSE?**  
Two checks. The wavelength, 0.123 nm, is about the spacing between atoms in a crystal – which is exactly why electrons diffract off crystals and why an electron microscope resolves what light never can. And the speed came out at 1.98% of the speed of light, comfortably low enough that ignoring relativity was justified.
